# Best Free Budgeting App 2026: 8 Options That Cost Nothing

The best free budgeting app for 2026 depends on how you manage money — but the good news is you don't have to spend money to start budgeting.

[YNAB costs $14.99/month](/blog/ynab-pricing-2026). Monarch Money runs $9.99/month. Those are excellent tools, but not everyone wants to pay a subscription just to track where their paycheck goes. The apps in this guide are genuinely free — not "free for 34 days" trials, not freemium bait-and-switches where the useful features are all paywalled. Real free tiers that real people actually use.

Some have limitations (they all do), and I'll be straightforward about what you're giving up. But for plenty of people — especially those just starting out — free is more than enough.

## Goodbudget — Best Free Envelope Budgeting App

Goodbudget takes the old-school cash envelope method and makes it digital. Instead of stuffing physical envelopes with cash, you assign money to virtual envelopes: Groceries, Rent, Gas, Fun Money, and so on. When the envelope is empty, you stop spending in that category.

**What's free:** Up to 10 envelopes, 1 account, 2 devices (you can share with a partner), and access to the full transaction history going back 1 year.

**What requires payment:** More than 10 envelopes, unlimited accounts, and 5-year transaction history require the Goodbudget Plus plan (around $10/month or $80/year).

**The honest take:** Ten envelopes is workable but tight. Most people end up wanting to split things like "Food" into "Groceries" and "Restaurants" separately, and suddenly you've used 2 of your 10 slots on just food. You'll need to be creative about grouping expenses. There's also no automatic bank sync on the free tier — you enter transactions manually, which some people actually prefer because it keeps you mindful of spending.

See more detail on [Goodbudget's pricing tiers](/blog/goodbudget-pricing-2026).

## EveryDollar — Best Free Zero-Based Budgeting App

EveryDollar comes from Ramsey Solutions (the Dave Ramsey people) and is built around zero-based budgeting — the idea that every dollar of income gets assigned a job until you reach $0. It's a structured method that works well for people who want to be intentional rather than just reactive with their money.

**What's free:** The core budgeting experience. You build your budget from scratch each month, categorize your spending, and track where every dollar goes. The interface is clean and straightforward.

**What requires payment:** Bank sync is the big one. The free version is entirely manual — you enter every transaction yourself. Automatic bank connection and a few premium features require Ramsey+ (a broader subscription that includes financial courses and other content).

**The honest take:** Manual entry sounds tedious, but it's actually a deliberate design choice. Dave Ramsey's whole philosophy involves being conscious of every dollar. If you're following the Baby Steps or just like a disciplined, active approach to budgeting, the free tier is genuinely complete. If you want your bank to populate transactions automatically, you'll need to pay.

For a full breakdown of what Ramsey+ includes, check the [EveryDollar pricing guide](/blog/everydollar-pricing-2026).

## Credit Karma Money — Best Free App With Bank Sync

When Mint shut down in late 2023, many users migrated to Credit Karma's money features, which Intuit (Mint's parent company) pushed users toward. Credit Karma Money offers a free checking account, automatic transaction tracking, and spending categorization — all at no cost.

**What's free:** Automatic transaction categorization, spending overview, free credit score monitoring, bill tracking, and a high-yield savings option. The credit monitoring is legitimately useful and genuinely free.

**What requires payment:** Nothing, technically. Credit Karma makes money through product recommendations (credit cards, loans) rather than subscriptions. That means you'll see offers throughout the app.

**The honest take:** It's not a budgeting app in the way Goodbudget or EveryDollar are — there's no explicit budget-building or envelope system. It's more of a financial overview tool: here's what you spent, here's your credit score, here's a credit card you might qualify for. For passive tracking, it's excellent. For active budget management, it's more limited.

## PocketGuard — Best Free Spending Snapshot App

PocketGuard answers one question faster than any other app: "How much money do I have left to spend right now?" Its signature "In My Pocket" number subtracts bills, savings goals, and necessities from your income and shows you what's left for discretionary spending.

**What's free:** The core "In My Pocket" calculation, limited bank account connections, basic spending categories, and bill tracking. You get the main idea of the app without paying.

**What requires payment:** PocketGuard Plus unlocks unlimited account connections, custom categories, debt payoff planning, and the ability to export your data. The free tier limits how many accounts you can link, which is a real constraint if you have checking, savings, and a couple of credit cards.

**The honest take:** If you only have one bank account and one credit card, the free tier covers you well. The "In My Pocket" calculation is genuinely useful and easy to understand at a glance. The limitations become frustrating as your financial life gets more complex.

## Fudget — Best Free App for Simple Budgeting Without the Fuss

Fudget is almost aggressively simple. It's a list-based budget — income minus expenses, done. There's no bank sync, no envelope system, no automatic categorization. Just a clean list where you add what came in and what went out.

**What's free:** The basic app on mobile. You can create budget lists, add items manually, and see your balance. It's bare-bones by design.

**What requires payment:** Fudget Pro adds multiple budget lists and a history view, but the free version gives you one active budget at a time.

**The honest take:** This one's for people who find traditional budgeting apps overwhelming. No accounts to connect, no setup process, no categories to argue about. You open the app, enter your income, enter your bills and expenses, and see what's left. Students, people new to budgeting, or anyone who just wants to know if they can afford something before buying it will find Fudget refreshingly approachable.

## Google Sheets (or Excel) — Best Free Option for DIY Budgeters

Not technically an app, but realistically one of the most used "budgeting tools" in existence. Google Sheets is free, works on every device, and can be as simple or as sophisticated as you want.

**What's free:** Everything. Google Sheets is completely free with a Google account.

**What requires payment:** Nothing, unless you want Microsoft 365 for Excel (which has a subscription cost). Google Sheets is free.

**How to get started:** Google's template gallery includes a monthly budget template. Vertex42 and other sites offer free budgeting spreadsheet templates specifically designed for budget tracking. You can find templates for zero-based budgeting, envelope budgeting, paycheck-by-paycheck budgeting, and more.

**The honest take:** The setup barrier is real. You're not going to open Google Sheets and have a working budget in 5 minutes the way you might with a dedicated app. But once you've built (or downloaded) a template that works for you, it's completely customizable — add a column for anything, create your own categories, track whatever you want. There's no company deciding which features are "premium." If you're comfortable with spreadsheets, this is hard to beat.

## Brigit — Best Free App for Balance Monitoring

Brigit is primarily marketed as a cash advance app, but its free tier offers genuine budgeting utility. You connect your bank account and Brigit monitors your balance, sends alerts before you might overdraft, and shows you a breakdown of your spending categories.

**What's free:** Bank account connection, balance monitoring, low-balance alerts, and spending insights. These features don't cost anything.

**What requires payment:** The cash advance features (getting up to $250 before payday) require a paid Plus plan. The free tier is purely budgeting and monitoring focused.

**The honest take:** If you've ever been hit by an unexpected overdraft fee, Brigit's free alert system alone might be worth installing. The spending breakdown is useful but not as detailed as dedicated budgeting apps. Think of the free tier as "financial safety net" rather than "full budgeting system."

More details on what the paid plan includes: [Brigit pricing breakdown](/blog/brigit-pricing-2026).

## Chime — Best Free Option If You're Open to Switching Banks

Chime isn't a budgeting app — it's a free online bank account. But it belongs on this list because its built-in features do a lot of what people use separate budgeting apps for, without any monthly fee.

**What's free:** The Chime checking account, automatic savings round-ups, spending notifications after every transaction, and spending categories in the app. SpotMe lets qualifying members overdraft up to $200 without fees.

**What requires payment:** Chime's basic account has no monthly fees. Some features have requirements (like SpotMe requiring regular direct deposits).

**The honest take:** This one has a catch — you have to actually bank with Chime. If you're already happy with your bank, this isn't a practical option. But if you're open to switching (or adding a second account), Chime's combination of free banking and built-in spending visibility is genuinely useful. The spending categories aren't as granular as dedicated budgeting apps, but for basic "where is my money going" awareness, it works.

## What You Give Up With Free Budgeting Apps

Being honest about the tradeoffs matters. Here's what most free budgeting apps limit or eliminate:

**Bank sync.** This is the biggest one. Manually entering transactions takes effort and most people eventually stop doing it. EveryDollar's free tier has no bank sync. Goodbudget's free tier has no bank sync. Fudget has no bank sync. If automatic transaction import is non-negotiable for you, your free options narrow significantly (Credit Karma Money and PocketGuard free tier are the main ones).

**Unlimited categories.** Goodbudget caps envelopes at 10. PocketGuard limits custom categories on the free tier. When your spending is genuinely complex — multiple income sources, side hustle expenses, irregular bills — these caps become real friction.

**Historical reports.** Paid apps let you see 12+ months of spending trends and compare month-over-month. Free tiers often limit history to a shorter window, which makes it harder to spot patterns.

**Multiple accounts.** If you have a checking account, savings account, and two credit cards, free tiers on several apps struggle to handle all four at once.

**Priority support.** If something goes wrong with a free account, you're usually looking at community forums or slow email support rather than fast help.

## When to Upgrade to a Paid App

Free tools are a great starting point. But there are real signs you've outgrown them:

- You're regularly hitting category or account limits and working around them awkwardly
- You have variable income and need proper irregular income tracking
- You're managing shared finances with a partner and need full sync between devices
- You want detailed year-over-year reports to understand your spending patterns
- You've tried free apps before and stopped using them — the friction of manual entry killed the habit

If any of these sound familiar, it might be worth looking at [free YNAB alternatives](/blog/best-free-ynab-alternatives-2026) that still cost less than $15/month but offer significantly more capability.

## FAQ

**Is there a completely free budgeting app with no ads?**
Google Sheets is completely free with no ads. Among dedicated budgeting apps, Goodbudget and EveryDollar are relatively low on advertising — they make money from paid upgrades rather than selling your data or showing ads. Credit Karma's free model is explicitly ad-supported through financial product recommendations.

**What happened to Mint? Is there a free replacement?**
Mint shut down in March 2024. For users who want a similar "automatic tracking with bank sync for free" experience, Credit Karma Money is the closest equivalent. It's from the same parent company (Intuit) and handles automatic categorization in a similar way.

**Can I budget for free without connecting my bank account?**
Yes. EveryDollar, Goodbudget (free tier), Fudget, and Google Sheets all work entirely with manual entry. Some people actually prefer this — entering transactions manually keeps you more aware of spending than watching numbers update automatically.

**What's the best free budgeting app for couples?**
Goodbudget's free tier allows 2 devices, which makes it the most functional option for couples on the free tier. Both partners can see the same envelopes and add transactions. EveryDollar also supports sharing, though the free tier limitations apply to both users.

**Do free budgeting apps sell my data?**
Policies vary. Credit Karma explicitly uses data to serve targeted financial product offers — that's their business model. Goodbudget and EveryDollar are more traditional freemium models that upsell to paid plans rather than monetizing user data. Always review the privacy policy of any app you connect to your bank account.

**Is a spreadsheet better than a free app?**
For control and customization, yes. For convenience and habit-building, usually no. Spreadsheets require more setup and discipline to maintain. Apps provide structure and reminders. The best tool is the one you'll actually use consistently — that's different for everyone.

## Verdict: Best Free App for Each Use Case

**You want envelope budgeting:** Goodbudget — the free tier is limited but the method is solid.

**You want zero-based budgeting:** EveryDollar — excellent structure, just enter transactions manually.

**You want automatic tracking without paying:** Credit Karma Money — full bank sync for free, with the understanding that the revenue model is financial product recommendations.

**You want the simplest possible option:** Fudget — open, type, done.

**You're comfortable with spreadsheets:** Google Sheets — maximum control, zero cost.

**You want spending alerts:** Brigit's free tier — particularly useful if overdraft fees have been a problem.

**You're open to switching banks:** Chime — free banking with built-in spending visibility.

The bottom line: free budgeting apps are genuinely useful, especially for people who are just starting to take their finances seriously. The main limitation is usually bank sync — and if that's a dealbreaker, Credit Karma Money is your best free option. For everyone else, pick the method that matches how your brain thinks about money (envelopes, zero-based, or simple tracking) and start there. A budgeting system you actually use beats a perfect system you abandon.
